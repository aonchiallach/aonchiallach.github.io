*Chapter 1: Europe's Imperial Juggernaut*

Late Ottoman period - industrialisation limited. The working class was small, imposing strict limitations on the organisation of left parties on Second International lines.

Large agrarian population, significant urban population around major towns, involved for the most part in trade and small manufacturing depending on cottage industries, artisanal class.

Most political parties and movements established my intellectual classes, either in positions of potential leadership on imperial level or on local level, many of them educated Ottoman notables.

New intellectual trends coming out of the Arab renaissance, Egyptian Muhammed Ali an important figure here. Rise of printing press, appearance of an intellectual class. Members of this class either engaged in intellectual debates or joined organisations which were underground; similar to clandestine organisations developing in Europe at the same time like the carobonari, quasi-Masonic.

Intellectuals confronting the nature of the Empire in crisis, at this stage countenancing a modernisation programme one political debate among Republicans would be limiting the power of the sultan, more provincial elements concerned particularly with the nature of the relationship between the provinces and the imperial centre; concern with constitutionalism and centralisation of the Empire

Arab political life also shaped by the introduction of elections, people begin joining parties with intellectual mandates especially after the 1908 constitutional revolution, the 1912 election The Freedom and Accord Party and the Committee of Union and Progress; almost every major town in Greater Syria and Iraq had people that were either accordists (decentralisers) or unionists (centralisation).

These debates become irrelevant after the collapse of the Empire; significant sociological adjustments accelerate; change in the nature of overall political challenges.

One of the big questions was how to face the challenge posed by Western imperialism, the Promethean leap industrially, economically and militarily,

Brits made sure Ali was driven out of Syria, secured the independence of Greece, their presence was being felt, first among merchants in the context among free trade agreements, debtors, and then taking over countries directly, as in Egypt, dual authority in Tunisia, British expansion in the context of the Napoleonic period in the Gulf, treaties with Oman that established British presence in the region; by the twentieth century almost everywhere is colonised

After the war the only parts which aren't directly colonised the core of modern-day Saudi Arabia, even though the Brits are very closely involved in al-Saud politics, inspires further colonial powers, Italy establishing footholds in Libya, colonial ventures in Somalia, Spanish entering Morrocan reef

When the Brits and the French first began to entering Arab political spheres many parts of this region were under Ottoman control; the Omani empire was not, this comes under British control via 'negotiation', which does not preclude coercion; trend of attempting to achieve colonial influence via local leaders, especially in the Gulf. This ossified political life; each leader was in their position because of imperial influence and were more powerful than any other indigenous faction. Huge expansionary campaign across the Gulf with leaders becoming subservient to Britain and introducing dynasties which endure to the present.

In some places rulers are removed altogether and a more radical colonial programme implemented, e.g. Algeria

The mandate system was one such form of governance; hugely racist, based on the notion of a civilising mission; different classes of mandates according to a perceived hierarchy of peoples with Europeans at the top (no mandates imposed) to African nations at the bottom (no hope of independence state even in the long term)

In the Gulf states local ruling or dominant families which existed were acknowledged and legitimised by the British. The Brits had promised the Sharifite of Mecca a Hashemite Arab kingdom that could exist after the Ottoman Empire had been dismembered

*Episode 2 - Birth of Arab Nationalism*

The Brits wanted for a major port in the Mediterranean that they could connect with Iraq and areas east of Suez - the canal was the most significant canal under their control, this made control over Palestine important, the divisions between Brits and French went against the wishes of the map that was drawn, the over-riding desire among the population was independence and unity

The border settlements which the Brits and the French imposed did not map onto any existing linguistic ethnic or racial identities; necessitated new identity formation

Motivations for Zionism among its architects manifold: material, religious, racial, economic: Balfour a virulent anti-Semite who regarded Jewish presence in Europe as a threat, Zionists leveraged these views to find international backers, finding it in the Brits.

Zionism represented itself as a moral rather than a commercial settler colonial project (it embraced the term)

Tensions contradictions continuities between Turkish nationalism and Arab nationalism

Revolts and resistance of varying scales throughout this period, met with brutal repression, in e.g. Iraq, Palestine + Syria, formative experience for the political cultures and movements in the region.

Brits respond to great Palestinian revolt 1936 - 39 with a white paper that seems to offer a roadmap for independence; implementation was unlikely to change the situation in any major way.

Influence of Russian Revolution in the formation of political parties, drawing on social changes; they operated locally, meaning their structures fell along the boundaries drawn by the Tans and French. This was also Comintern policy

Some, such as the preacher Izz ad-Din al-Qassam mobilised religion to the end of anti-imperialist goals, realising that force was the only means of resisting colonialism, starts a campaign on guerilla warfare based on the working class and peasantry from Haifa

Ba'ath party and its core ideology developed first by Michel Aflaq and Salah al-Din al-Bitar and they are its founders with Zaki al-Arsuzi as an assistance.

Zaki al-Arsuzi born in an ethnically diverse parts of northern Syria, historically of racialised violence, contested by the Turks Aflaq and al-Bitar from Damascus, their formative political experience was the great revolt in Syria 1925 - 7, followed by the experience of studying in the metropole, contact with the left in France, communist party, until the Communist Party prioritises the anti-fascist alliance over the colonial question, was anti-communist but had a certain commitment to socialism.

*Episode 3: The Post-Colonial Arab State System*

Changed situation post war French were the weakest power; France could no longer meaningfully intervene in Syrian politics

Colonial powers adjusted policies in response to revolts, ensuring the continuity of groups or individuals who may have opposed their presence but were willing to work with them; more indirect form of colonialism.

King Abdullah I initially planned to expand into Syria, cultivated relationship with leaders of the Syrian independence movements, gave them refuge, establish a strong national coalition and an Arab kingdom under his rule across Syria. When it became clear that there was no support for this his best bet for territorial expansion was Palestine, where he builds a power base among a number of influential families along the West Bank to convince the Brits he could govern it in a manner suitable to their interests. 

Kurds clash with the British Empire, regard themselves as having been left out by the state structure the Brits left them with.

French present themselves to the Christians, mostly Catholic, in Lebanon as their protectors, larger Syrian space predominantly Sunni Muslim, this diversity was manageable for years, but the French vision was to separate the Christian minority from the rest of the territory.

Lebanese Shia Muslims disenfranchised - poor peasantry and working class, versus Maronite bourgeoise Maronite financiers, cities predominantly Sunni Muslims, Orthodox Christians tended also wealthier cohort

Gulf subject to British colonialism very early on, for a long time managed by the British government in India, designed as a buffer zone, British Monroe Doctrine, no other power is allowed to come into this area, we will manage the major trading routes and we'll give the local ruling structures domestic authorities within those areas, the Brits tended to operate through series of alliances w existing tribal leaders and structures, more centralising structures around the al-Saud, sometimes they had conflicts with them and deliberations but always managed to prevent them from encroaching on coastal areas

Without the Brits establishing themselves in Kuwait, Bahrain, Qatar, Oman, which later became the United Arab Emirates these places would have been parts of Saudi Arabia, without the British backing them ruling families would have found it very difficult to hold onto power

The Saudis had a local movement behind them, a religious ideology behind them but it was again the Brits that allowed them to formalise their control and expand over the Arabian peninsula especially when they failed to protect the Hejaz from falling under Saudi rule, they left Hussain who had rebelled against the Ottomans

Hejaz portion of Saudi Arabia where Mecca and Medina are, the core is around holy cities of Mecca and Medina, main port Jeddah long under Ottoman control, had an Ottoman culture, the Saudi state begins to transform everything around it, appropriate it and develops through appropriation, its a commonwealth by acquisition and coercion but as it swallows up everything around it it also acquires some of the features of that which it assimilates.
